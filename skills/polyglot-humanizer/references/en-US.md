# en-US language pack guidance

Edit for natural US English without turning the result into a rigid “anti-AI” style.

## Strong patterns

### Staged openers

Openers such as “Here's the thing,” “Let's dive in,” or “What you need to know is” are suspicious when they only announce a routine point. Start with the point.

### False contrast

`not X but Y`, `not just X but Y`, or an invented objection is a strong signal when X was never a live possibility. Keep real corrections and real tradeoffs.

### Dramatic closers

One-line paragraphs such as “That's the real win” or “Let that sink in” are usually filler if they repeat the preceding sentence.

### Forced triads

Three parallel nouns, benefits, examples, or sentences should exist because the meaning has three parts, not because three sounds complete.

### Inflated significance

Phrases that frame routine facts as pivotal, transformative, groundbreaking, or a testament to something should be replaced with the concrete fact unless the source actually supports that significance.

## Contextual patterns

### Dash density

Em dashes are legitimate English punctuation. Multiple dashes across a short passage can be a model habit. Match the writer's sample when available; otherwise choose punctuation based on the relationship between clauses.

### Stock model vocabulary

Words such as `delve`, `robust`, `seamless`, `leverage`, `landscape`, `pivotal`, `intricate`, `underscore`, and `showcase` are weak signals. Keep them when precise; rewrite only when they cluster with other generic habits.

### Business jargon

Replace abstract verbs with concrete actors/actions when the source provides them. Do not manufacture specificity.

### Repeated sentence openings

Several sentences beginning with the same pronoun or noun can sound mechanically generated, but rhetorical repetition may be intentional.

### Formatting habits

Avoid unnecessary bold labels, repeated “Key takeaway” boxes, and identical section templates when plain prose is clearer.

## Register

Technical, legal, academic, and reference writing can legitimately be neutral, repetitive, hedged, and structured. Prefer correctness over forced conversationality.
